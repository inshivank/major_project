# Antibiotics: appropriate use

Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/antimicrobial-resistance

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They do not work against viruses, so they do not help colds, influenza, most sore throats, most coughs, or viral gastroenteritis. Using antibiotics when they are not needed exposes the patient to side effects and drives antimicrobial resistance, one of the biggest threats to global health.

Antibiotics may be appropriate for confirmed or strongly suspected bacterial infections such as some pneumonias, urinary tract infections, skin infections, and certain throat infections. The choice of antibiotic, dose, and duration depend on the infection, the person, and local resistance patterns.

When prescribed, take the medicine at the stated times and complete the course as instructed. Do not save leftovers for later or share antibiotics with others. If side effects occur, such as rash, severe diarrhoea, or difficulty breathing, seek advice promptly.

Resistance develops when bacteria survive exposure to antibiotics, often because treatment is stopped early or antibiotics are used unnecessarily. Resistant infections are harder to treat and can be life-threatening. Infection prevention, including hand hygiene, vaccination, and safe food handling, reduces the need for antibiotics in the first place.

For a cold or flu, focus on rest, fluids, and symptom relief. Ask a clinician or pharmacist what is appropriate. Never pressure a clinician for antibiotics when they are not indicated, and never self-medicate with leftover tablets.
