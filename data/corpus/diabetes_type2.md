# Type 2 diabetes: overview

Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body becomes resistant to insulin and gradually produces less of it, causing blood glucose to rise. It is the most common form of diabetes and is linked to excess body weight, physical inactivity, family history, and increasing age, although it can affect people of any age.

Early type 2 diabetes often causes no symptoms. When symptoms do appear they include increased thirst, frequent urination, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, and recurrent infections. Because symptoms can be mild, many people are diagnosed through screening blood tests.

Diagnosis is based on blood tests: a fasting glucose, an oral glucose tolerance test, or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). A clinician confirms the diagnosis and usually repeats the test unless the result is unambiguous.

Management combines healthy eating, regular physical activity, weight reduction where appropriate, and medicines. Metformin is commonly the first medicine. Some people eventually need insulin or other injectable therapies. Regular monitoring of blood pressure, kidney function, cholesterol, and eyes and feet helps prevent complications.

Persistently high glucose damages blood vessels and nerves and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, vision loss, and foot ulcers. Good control of glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol substantially reduces these risks, which is why regular follow-up matters.

People with diabetes should seek prompt advice if they develop very high glucose with vomiting, abdominal pain, and drowsiness, or if they have a foot wound that is not healing. Hypoglycaemia, with shaking, sweating, confusion, and hunger, is an emergency when severe and requires fast-acting sugar followed by medical review.
