# Common cold and influenza

Source: https://www.cdc.gov/flu/about/index.html

Colds and influenza are both viral infections of the respiratory tract, but they differ in severity. A cold usually develops slowly and causes a runny or blocked nose, sneezing, sore throat, cough, and mild tiredness. Influenza tends to start suddenly with fever, chills, muscle aches, headache, dry cough, and marked fatigue.

Both spread through droplets released when an infected person coughs or sneezes, and through hands and surfaces contaminated with the virus. Regular hand washing, covering coughs and sneezes, and staying home while unwell reduce transmission.

Most people recover from a cold in about a week to ten days. Flu symptoms usually improve within five to seven days, although cough and tiredness can linger for two to three weeks. Rest, fluids, and simple pain relief are the mainstays of care. Antibiotics do not work against viruses and should not be used for colds or flu unless a clinician diagnoses a secondary bacterial infection.

Antiviral medicines for influenza can shorten illness if started within the first two days, and are especially important for people at higher risk: older adults, pregnant women, young children, and people with chronic heart, lung, kidney, or metabolic disease. Annual influenza vaccination lowers the risk of severe illness.

Seek urgent care for difficulty breathing, chest pain, confusion, a fever that returns after improving, dehydration, or worsening symptoms in a high-risk person. In children, watch for fast breathing, poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, or a rash that does not fade under pressure.
