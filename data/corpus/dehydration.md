# Dehydration in adults

Source: https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/153363

Dehydration happens when the body loses more fluid than it takes in. It is common with fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, hot weather, strenuous exercise, and reduced fluid intake. Older adults and people taking diuretics are at higher risk because the sensation of thirst and the ability to conserve fluid decline with age.

Early signs include a dry mouth, dark yellow urine, reduced urination, tiredness, headache, and dizziness on standing. More severe dehydration causes confusion, rapid heartbeat, low blood pressure, sunken eyes, and reduced skin elasticity. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency, particularly in older adults and young children.

For mild dehydration, drink water or an oral rehydration solution in small sips frequently. Oral rehydration solutions contain the right balance of salt and sugar and are more effective than water alone when losses from vomiting or diarrhoea are significant. Avoid alcohol and very sugary drinks, which can worsen dehydration.

If vomiting prevents keeping fluids down, try small amounts every few minutes. Seek clinical advice if symptoms persist, if urine output drops markedly, or if the person cannot keep any fluid down. Infants, older adults, and people with kidney or heart disease need earlier assessment because fluid balance is more delicate.

Thirst is not always a reliable guide, especially in older adults. Encourage regular drinking during illness and hot weather, and check urine colour as a simple indicator. In hot climates and during exercise, replace both water and electrolytes.

This information is general education and does not replace assessment by a clinician, especially when dehydration is severe or the cause is unclear.
