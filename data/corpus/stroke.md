# Stroke: recognising and acting fast

Source: https://www.stroke.org/en/about-stroke/stroke-symptoms

A stroke occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, either by a clot or by bleeding. Brain cells begin to die within minutes, so treatment is time-critical. Several effective treatments, including clot-dissolving medicine and clot retrieval, work best when given within hours of symptom onset.

The FAST test helps people remember the warning signs. Face: is one side drooping, or does the smile look uneven? Arms: can the person raise both arms, or does one drift downwards? Speech: is speech slurred, garbled, or hard to understand? Time: if any of these are present, call emergency services immediately and note the time symptoms began.

Other possible signs include sudden loss of vision or double vision, sudden severe headache with no known cause, dizziness or loss of balance, confusion, and numbness on one side of the body. Symptoms may be brief and then improve, which can indicate a transient ischaemic attack, or mini-stroke. This is still an emergency because it often precedes a major stroke.

Do not wait for symptoms to settle. Do not give food or drink in case swallowing is affected. Do not drive the person to hospital yourself unless emergency services cannot be reached. Tell the medical team when the symptoms started and what medicines the person takes, especially blood thinners.

Recovery varies widely and often requires physiotherapy, speech and language therapy, and occupational therapy. Controlling blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, and smoking reduces the risk of a further stroke. Rehabilitation begun early improves outcomes.
