# Asthma

Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/asthma

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways. The airways narrow and become sensitive to triggers such as allergens, cold air, exercise, smoke, and respiratory infections, causing wheezing, cough, chest tightness, and shortness of breath.

Symptoms often vary over time and may be worse at night or early in the morning. A clinician confirms asthma using the history plus lung function tests, such as spirometry with a bronchodilator response, and sometimes a peak flow diary or allergy testing.

Treatment has two parts. A reliever inhaler opens the airways quickly during symptoms. A preventer inhaler, usually containing a low-dose corticosteroid, reduces inflammation when used regularly. Many guidelines now recommend combination inhalers for both relief and maintenance. Inhaler technique matters as much as the prescription, so it should be checked at every review.

Most people with well-controlled asthma can live fully active lives. A written action plan describing what to do when symptoms worsen helps prevent attacks. Avoiding known triggers, stopping smoking, and keeping vaccinations up to date reduce flare-ups.

Signs of a severe asthma attack include symptoms that do not improve after using a reliever inhaler, difficulty speaking in full sentences, fast breathing, blue lips, drowsiness, or exhaustion. This is an emergency: call emergency services, sit the person upright, and use the reliever inhaler as instructed while waiting for help.

A person whose reliever is needed more than three times a week, whose sleep is disturbed by symptoms, or who has had an attack in the past year should be reviewed promptly, as this suggests inadequate control.
